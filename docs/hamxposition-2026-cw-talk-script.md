## The story in one sentence

By the end, a Technician should see CW not as an obsolete test or an elite skill, but as a practical, portable operating option they can learn sound by sound and begin using through one small, safe contact.

## Narrative map

1. **My first attempt taught me the recording, not the sounds.** I was sure I had learned Morse; the exam showed that I had memorized one cassette’s sequence instead.
2. **Portable operating gave me a reason to learn it for real.** CW gives a Technician more HF access and more options when power, noise, weight, or public-space operating matters.
3. **Learning works when the practice teaches sound recognition.** Avoid counting, choose a repeatable routine, and move toward useful on-air material.
4. **The first contact can be made small and safe.** POTA provides a predictable language island; listening, recovery words, and a patient partner remove the feeling of an exam.
5. **The station is smaller than the fear around it.** A complete portable station is only five pieces, and none needs to be exotic.
6. **POTA is scaffolding, not a ceiling.** Start with one repeated exchange, then add one human detail at a time.

## Slide 2 — The first time, Morse was a requirement

**TARGET:** 1:20 | **CUMULATIVE:** 2:10

“I first met Morse code in 1998, when it was still a licensing requirement. I was completely confident that I was going to walk out as a Technician Plus. I had studied. I had one Gordon West cassette, and I had listened to it over and over. I knew that tape. I was sure I had it licked.

“I passed the Technician written exam. Then the examiners did something deeply unfair: they played the Morse characters in a different order from the cassette. I failed the Morse element.

“I went back, learned enough to pass, earned KB9SMK, and upgraded to General in 1999. But once Morse had done its job as a test, I stopped using it. When I came back to amateur radio in 2025, almost all of it was gone.

“I had worked hard, but I had learned the recording more than I had learned Morse. I could predict what came next; I could not yet recognize each sound on its own.”

**Transition:** “For years after that, Morse still felt like a test rather than something I might actually want to use.”

## Slide 3 — CW is useful!

**TARGET:** 1:10 | **CUMULATIVE:** 3:20

[Quick show of hands.]

“When you hear ‘Morse code,’ who thinks old? Outdated? Something that used to be on a license test?”

[Acknowledge the room.]

“That is how I thought about it for years. But amateur radio is often called ‘the hobby of hobbies.’ We can build equipment, chase satellites, join local nets, operate from parks, contest, experiment with antennas, or spend years exploring one tiny technical corner.

“CW is another branch of that hobby. It gives us another way to make a contact when signals are weak, power is limited, the whole station has to fit in a small bag, or we want to operate quietly around other people. It is not better than voice, digital modes, or satellites. It is one more thing you can try—and it may be the thing that makes you want to get on the air.”

## Slide 4 — The second time, POTA gave me a reason

**TARGET:** 1:10 | **CUMULATIVE:** 4:30

“When I came back to amateur radio in 2025, portable operating—especially Parks on the Air—gave me a reason to learn CW for real.

“I watched people carry a tiny radio, a key, and a wire into beautiful places and make contacts with very little power. It looked like a useful outdoor skill, and honestly, it looked fun. That was why I wanted to learn it.

“I made my first CW contact on April 12 of this year, and my first all-CW POTA activation two weeks later. So I am not standing here as somebody who has done this for forty years. I am a recent learner. I still find an ordinary conversational QSO much harder than a POTA exchange.

“What I can give you is a fresh map of the part between ‘that seems impossible’ and ‘I just made a contact.’”

**Transition:** “Let me show you a few of the reasons it clicked for me.”

## Slide 5 — CW gives you more options with less station

**TARGET:** 2:15 | **CUMULATIVE:** 6:45

“First: weak or noisy signals. A CW signal occupies a very small part of the receiver’s passband. If I can narrow the receiver around that one tone, I reject a great deal of noise that I do not need.”

[Do not attempt an audio or live-receiver demo in the room. Use the slide’s “weak signals” and “narrow filters reduce noise” lines as the visual anchor.]

“On a receiver, switching from a wide filter to a narrow one makes much of the surrounding hiss fall away while the CW tone remains. I did not make the transmitter on the other end stronger. I gave my receiver less unwanted noise to listen to.

“Here is the simplified arithmetic. If the background noise is spread evenly across the frequencies we are listening to, a 3-kilohertz filter admits about 7.8 dB more noise than a 500-hertz filter. Compared with 50 hertz, the difference is about 17.8 dB. That does **not** mean CW is automatically eighteen decibels better than SSB. Real signals, fading, tuning, and decoding are more complicated. The point is that a narrow filter can remove a lot of noise from around a CW signal and make that one tone much easier to hear.

“Portability is another reason. Small CW radios can use small batteries. The entire station can fit into a side bag. With headphones, I can sit quietly enough that a family of deer can wander within thirty feet instead of being the guy shouting at the trees.

“And CW can simply be fun: a physical key, a human rhythm, and the satisfaction of pulling a call sign out of what sounded like noise a moment earlier.”

**Transition:** “For a Technician, it unlocks a whole new world of HF.”

## Slide 6 — Your Technician license opens four CW windows

**TARGET:** 1:15 | **CUMULATIVE:** 8:00

“Your Technician license already gives you CW privileges on four HF bands: 80, 40, 15, and 10 meters. There is no Morse exam to pass, and the limit in these segments is 200 watts PEP.

“Ten meters is the familiar Technician path onto HF because it includes voice and digital privileges as well as CW. On 80, 40, and 15, CW is the **only** HF mode a Technician can transmit on.

“So you can experience four HF bands before upgrading, including how they change with time of day and distance. General and Extra open much more spectrum, but CW grows with the upgrade; it is not left behind. And although the Technician limit here is 200 watts PEP, these contacts can be made with dramatically less.”

**Transition:** “So CW is available to you now. The next question is how to practice without accidentally memorizing the cassette tape.”

## Slide 7 — A bad practice method can teach the wrong skill

**TARGET:** 1:00 | **CUMULATIVE:** 9:00

“My cassette story is funny now, but it illustrates something important: practice is not automatically good just because we worked hard at it. Practice teaches the thing we repeatedly do.

“Three traps are counting dots and dashes, building a heroic practice plan we avoid after three days, and remaining in random-character drills until some imaginary day when we feel perfect.

“We can become good at drills while never practicing our own call, a signal report, or what to do after somebody answers.

“If progress stalls, do not immediately conclude that you cannot learn CW. Ask what skill your practice is actually rehearsing.”

**Transition:** “Let’s talk about some of these pitfalls. The first trap—counting—is the one that can follow us the longest.”

---

## Slide 8 — The biggest mistake is counting dits and dahs

**TARGET:** 4:00 | **CUMULATIVE:** 13:00

“If I play a character very slowly, it is almost impossible not to count it. You hear short, short, long, and your brain performs a lookup. That can work at very low speed, but the translation step becomes a bottleneck as the code gets faster.

“What we want is direct recognition. When you hear a doorbell or a familiar ringtone, you do not count its pieces. You recognize its overall sound. Morse characters can become the same kind of sound object.”

[Sidetone demo, part 1: with no antenna connected and the radio in TEST or practice mode, send the announced characters `B`, `L`, and `Q` slowly enough to count. Ask what people found themselves doing. If the direct room-audio connection fails, hold the room microphone near the radio speaker.]

“Now I will play those characters with each character formed at roughly 20 to 25 words per minute, but with more empty space between characters. The exercise is still slow. The character itself keeps its rhythm.”

[Sidetone demo, part 2: send `B`, `L`, and `Q` at roughly 22 WPM character speed, pausing for two or three seconds between characters.]

“That extra spacing is often called Farnsworth spacing. It gives you time to answer without deforming the sound you are trying to learn.

“Twenty to twenty-five words per minute is not a sacred number. It is a useful starting range because counting becomes inconvenient. The principle is more important than the number: keep the character rhythm intact and slow the exercise with space.

“Writing down the character you recognized is fine. The trap is hearing Morse, converting it into dots and dashes, and only then looking up the letter.”

**Transition:** “A sound-based method matters, but an app cannot give us everything.”

## Slide 9 — Classes add people, feedback, and accountability

**TARGET:** 1:15 | **CUMULATIVE:** 14:15

“One excellent way to learn is with other people.

“The Long Island CW Club runs ongoing small-group online classes at several levels. You can enter the path and keep moving at your pace. I joined before I was really ready to commit, attended a few classes, and knew the community was there when I needed it.

“CWops sponsors CW Academy. Its sessions combine two coached meetings each week with daily practice. I took Fundamentals this spring. My instructor and classmates did something an app cannot: they made confusion normal. Everybody missed characters. Everybody sent imperfectly. We could get feedback and try again.

“A teacher may notice that you are counting, that your spacing is uneven, or that your hand is tense. A class also puts practice on the calendar.

“I am not ranking the programs. CW Innovations is another path, especially for conversational rhythm and head copy. Choose the structure that fits the skill you want and the schedule you can honestly keep.”

**Transition:** “When you are practicing alone, match the tool to the kind of time you actually have.”

## Slide 10 — Match the practice tool to the time you have

**TARGET:** 2:15 | **CUMULATIVE:** 16:30

“People often ask, ‘What is the best app?’ I think that question is slightly wrong. The better question is, ‘What sound-based practice can I repeat in a real part of my day?’

“When I could look at my phone and respond, Morse Mania gave me focused, interactive drills. It was useful for adding a few characters and making progress visible.

“A lot of my available time was in the car—school runs, errands, an hour or more on some days. Morse Code Ninja has audio, podcast, and video lessons that fit that time. I could hear a new character, hear it mixed with earlier characters, and keep building without touching a screen. That fit the life I actually had.

“When you want to practice with a real key, Vail Morse provides a browser decoder and an internet repeater. The optional Vail Adapter connects a straight key or paddle by USB and gives you responsive sidetone.

“You do not need all of these. Pick one that fits a recurring moment in your week. Use it long enough to learn from it. If you are still using it next week, that is much more important than whether somebody online called it the optimal method.”

**Transition:** “That leads to the least impressive and most effective training plan I know.”

## Slide 11 — Fifteen minutes you repeat beats an hour you avoid

**TARGET:** 0:45 | **CUMULATIVE:** 17:15

“Fifteen minutes is not a biological law. It is a promise small enough to protect.

“A short daily session keeps the sounds returning before they become strangers. On a good day, keep going. On a bad day, fifteen minutes still preserves the habit. My own mix included listening while driving and short, focused sessions with a phone or key.”

**Transition:** “So let’s turn that fifteen-minute habit into a very modest first week.”

## Slide 12 — Your first seven days: build the habit

**TARGET:** 1:30 | **CUMULATIVE:** 18:45

“This is not a seven-day promise of fluency. It is a plan for becoming somebody who practices—a useful goal for Morse, and for almost any skill or hobby.

“On day one, choose one sound-based trainer. Set the character speed around 20 to 25 words per minute, add extra spacing, and stop shopping for methods.

“On days two through six, practice for fifteen minutes. Keep the character set small enough that recognition can form. Some days will feel worse than the day before. That is normal; memory is not a progress bar.

“On day seven, listen to a POTA activation. Do not transmit yet. Notice whether any sound jumps out: a familiar letter, a number, `CQ`, a state, maybe part of a call.

“Then keep building. When your own call and the expected exchange are reliable, arrange one patient contact.

“The important design choice is that real radio enters the process immediately. We do not hide in drills until the entire band sounds easy. We let listening give the practice a purpose.”

**Transition:** “When should you get on the air?”

---

## Slide 13 — Do not wait for perfect copy

**TARGET:** 1:30 | **CUMULATIVE:** 20:15

“Before your first contact, I would like you to be able to recognize your own call, send your call comfortably, copy the expected report and location, and ask for help.

“That is the list. Perfect copy is not on it.

“Two recovery tools change the emotional shape of a contact. `AGN?` means ‘again?’ `QRS PSE` means ‘slower, please.’ You can also send `CALL?` when the callsign is what you missed.

“A missed character is not proof that the QSO failed. Asking for a repeat is part of the protocol. Experienced operators ask for repeats. Noise, fading, interference, and human fingers happen to everyone.

“Your first QSO should not test every CW skill at once. It should test a small, prepared exchange with a recovery plan. If you can recognize when somebody is calling you, send your own call, exchange the expected information, and ask for a repeat, you have enough skill to learn something on the air.”

**Transition:** “POTA is especially good for that bridge because it creates a small, predictable island of language.”

---

## Slide 14 — POTA is the best first on-air classroom

**TARGET:** 1:30 | **CUMULATIVE:** 21:45

“I am going to make a strong claim with one qualification: for many people, POTA is the best first on-air CW classroom.

“The activator repeats a short pattern. Hunters already know why the station is calling. A successful activation has a visible ten-contact goal. POTA does not require one formal exchange, but callsign, signal report, and location are common.

“That creates what language learners sometimes call a language island: a small situation where you know most of what is likely to happen.

“As a hunter, you can listen to the same activator complete contact after contact. As an activator, every caller gives you another repetition of almost the same sequence. The context carries some of the mental load while your ear catches up.

“POTA is not magic, and it is not the only way to begin. A scheduled club contact can do the same thing. Its advantage is a large supply of short, purposeful, repeated exchanges.”

**Transition:** “The safest first move is not transmitting. It is learning how to find and understand one easy signal.”

## Slide 15 — Find one easy signal before you transmit

**TARGET:** 1:30 | **CUMULATIVE:** 23:15

“Start on the POTA spots page. Choose a CW spot inside one of the Technician segments we saw earlier.

“Then tune near the listed frequency. I said ‘near’ because a spot is a hint, not confirmation. It may be stale, mistyped, or slightly off frequency. The callsign you hear on the air is what controls.

“Listen until you can verify the activator’s call. Then listen through two complete contacts. You are learning the activator’s rhythm: when they ask for calls, where they put the report, whether they send a state or park reference, and how they end one contact before inviting the next.

“Do not feel rushed by the pileup. You can simply listen. There will be another contact and another activation.

“When the pattern begins to feel unsurprising, you have turned a line on a website into a station you actually understand.”

**Transition:** “Now the hunter’s job is only five steps.”

---

## Slide 16 — Hunt before you activate

**TARGET:** 1:30 | **CUMULATIVE:** 24:45

“First, listen until you know the activator’s call.

“Second, when they invite callers, send your own call once. You do not need to send their call back to them. They know who they are.

“Third, listen for your call. If they answer you, copy the report and location—or whatever information that activator is using.

“Fourth, send a report and your location. If they ask for something different, follow what you hear; POTA does not mandate one sacred exchange.

“Fifth, log the contact. Then stay for the next hunter. Hearing the pattern succeed for somebody else reinforces what just happened to you.

“Hunting first matters because it lets you experience the exchange with only one decision point: when to send your call. You are not yet choosing a frequency, calling CQ, sorting multiple callers, or deciding when the activation is finished.”

**Transition:** “Let’s make that short exchange visible and work through it together.”

## Slide 17 — Demo: let’s work a POTA contact

**TARGET:** 4:00 | **CUMULATIVE:** 28:45

[Invite the preselected, briefed volunteer. Give them the hunter card showing `N1ABC` and `MA`. Use sidetone only—no RF. If the volunteer is unavailable or uncomfortable sending, key both sides yourself and have them read the hunter lines aloud.]

“I am the activator, N1RWJ. You are the hunter, N1ABC, in Massachusetts. Before we send any Morse, here is the entire conversation in English.

“I say, ‘Calling any station for Parks on the Air; this is N1RWJ; go ahead.’ You send your call. I send your call back so you know I chose you, then I give you a report and Rhode Island. You give me a report and Massachusetts. I close with ‘thank you, 73, from N1RWJ,’ followed by dit dit. That is the contact.”

[Point to the abbreviations.]

“`DE` means ‘from.’ `K` means ‘go ahead.’ `TU` is ‘thank you.’ `5NN` is the common shortened form of 599. `73` means ‘best regards,’ and dit dit is a friendly final sign-off.”

[Walk through or send the displayed exchange at a comfortable speed. Pause after each line and translate it.]

“Now let’s make something go wrong, because recovery is the skill that makes this safe.”

[Deliberately miss the volunteer’s call or report. Send `AGN?` or `CALL?`; on another pass, demonstrate `QRS PSE`.]

“Notice that the contact did not collapse. We moved into a recovery state, repeated one piece, and continued.

“The exact wording will vary from operator to operator. `potacw.guide` has a complete line-by-line field guide and a customizable cheat sheet. The goal is not to memorize my script. It is to recognize the shape of a short exchange and know how to repair it.”

**Transition:** “That is what the contact looks like. My first real one was arranged in advance.”

## Slide 18 — Your first contact can be arranged

**TARGET:** 1:45 | **CUMULATIVE:** 30:30

“My first CW contact was arranged.

“A week earlier, Brian, K1NW, had heard me activating a local park on voice. He realized he was nearby, came over, and we spent time operating and talking. I mentioned that I was learning CW but had not made a contact.

“Brian was about to travel through the Southeast. He said, essentially, ‘If you hear me, send your call. I will slow down. We can repeat anything you miss. We will take as long as you need.’

“On April 12, at 23:47 UTC, I heard him activating Jones Lake State Park in North Carolina. I sent my call. He slowed down, repeated what I needed, and the contact went in the log.

“Arranging the contact did not make it fake. It made the learning environment safe. An Elmer, club member, instructor, sibling, or friend can choose a clear signal, agree on the exchange, and remove the fear that the other operator will disappear while you are asking for a repeat.”

**Transition:** “Once I had one safe contact, the next step arrived much sooner than I expected.”

## Slide 19 — Fourteen days from first QSO to calling CQ

**TARGET:** 0:45 | **CUMULATIVE:** 31:15

“Fourteen days after that contact with Brian, I went to Scarborough North State Beach and attempted an all-CW POTA activation.

“I used five watts. I made thirteen contacts. That was fifty-four days earlier than the Florida-trip goal I had set for myself.

“Fourteen days is not a benchmark. The important sequence is underneath it: one safe contact, several bounded exchanges, then the same exchange with the roles reversed while I called CQ.

“Getting on the air did not end the learning. It made the next practice session more specific.”

**Transition:** “At this point people reasonably ask, ‘Fine—but what equipment does this actually require?’ Notice how late gear appears in this talk. That is intentional.”

## Slide 20 — The complete station is five things

**TARGET:** 2:30 | **CUMULATIVE:** 33:45

[Place a radio, paddle, battery, and log where the audience can see them. There will be no antenna in the room; use the slide for that system. Show only enough gear to make each part concrete, and keep feature comparisons brief.]

“A complete portable CW station is five systems.

“One: a radio.

“Two: a key or paddle.

“Three: an antenna.

“Four: power.

“Five: a log—paper, phone, or computer.

“That is the station. Headphones are very useful in public, but they are not a mysterious sixth subsystem.

“For the radio, the important starting question is simple: does it cover the band and CW, and can it accept your key? These radios show different tradeoffs, not different levels of legitimacy. Borrow first if you can; after a few contacts, you will know whether weight, a tuner, or a larger display actually matters to you.

[Show the paddle.]

“A paddle asks the radio’s electronic keyer for dits and dahs; the keyer supplies the consistent timing. This Zippy paddle shows how small that interface can be. You have already heard the radio sidetone, so I am not going to turn this into another demo.

“The antenna is the part that will not be on this table. When I have room, a wire is light, efficient, and inexpensive. A whip is quicker and more self-contained, but especially on lower bands it asks us to accept more compromise. A tuner can make the impedance acceptable to the radio; it cannot repeal physics.

[Show the battery and log.]

“Small radios can use small batteries. The log can be paper, a phone, or a computer. None of these five systems needs to be exotic, and you do not have to buy the final version of any of them before making a contact.

“For more detail, ‘Choosing Your First Ham Radio’ is Saturday at 11, ‘Ham2K Portable Logger’ is Saturday at 1, and ‘Stealthy All-Band HF from Attics and Small Spaces’ is Saturday at 3.”

**Transition:** “Now we have a learning path, a first exchange, and a complete station. The last question is where that path goes.”

## Slide 21 — POTA is the gateway, not the destination

**TARGET:** 1:15 | **CUMULATIVE:** 35:00

“POTA is valuable because it supplies scaffolding. Scaffolding is supposed to help us build something beyond itself.

“Once the POTA exchange feels comfortable, add one human detail. Send your name. On another contact, add your QTH. Prepare one small question: `RIG?`, `ANT?`, or `WX?`

“Do not leap directly from a three-line POTA exchange to a twenty-minute ragchew and conclude that you failed when you lose the thread. Keep most of the structure familiar and add one unpredictable element at a time.

“This is the current edge of my own learning. I can run a CW POTA activation with much more confidence than I can have an ordinary conversation. My goal is to carry a tiny station somewhere that is not a park, throw a wire into a tree, call CQ, exchange a name and place and one real thought, and pack up smiling.”

**Transition:** “So let me leave you with a much smaller next action than ‘learn Morse code.’”

### Delivery reminders

- Keep the personal history to the opening and the two first-contact proof points. Every personal detail should earn an audience lesson.
- Assume some people arrived for this session alone; make every reference to another Toolkit session understandable without having attended it.
- Say “one useful lane,” not “the best mode.” The invitation works better without mode hierarchy.
- Pause after each demo change. Let the room hear or see the difference before explaining it.
- When describing mistakes, say “we” more often than “beginners.”
- Avoid promising a speed or a timeline. The talk promises a path, not fluency in seven or fourteen days.
- End on the exact final line before opening questions: “One sound. One call sign. One safe first contact.”
