## Opening and resources — 0:16

Before we get started, that QR code goes to a page on my website with every useful link I could think of: everything I reference in the slides, additional resources, and the slides themselves. The same link will appear again at the end.

I’m Rob, N1RWJ. We are going to talk about learning CW—what hams usually call Morse code sent over the air. Technically, Morse code is the thing we send and continuous wave is how the radio sends it, but I will use the terms interchangeably today.

There are three questions I want to cover. Why should I learn it when I do not have to? How can I learn without falling into the common traps? And how can I start making contacts?

## The cassette I learned too well — 1:40

The first time I tried to learn Morse code, I knew it perfectly—and then failed the test.

Back in 1998, I was working in a library. I found an amateur-radio book, became interested, and started studying for the Technician license. I learned that passing a Morse test would give me additional HF privileges. The test was five words per minute, so I found a Gordon West cassette and listened to it while driving back and forth to school.

I had it nailed. I could call out each character before the recorded voice gave the answer.

There was no convenient online test search then. The closest exam I found was in Chicago, a few hours away, so I persuaded my grandfather to drive me there. I practiced in the car and felt completely ready. I did well on the written exam. Then the volunteer examiner did something deeply unfair: he sent the Morse characters in a different order from the cassette.

I had memorized the sequence on the recording. I had not learned to recognize each sound independently. I failed badly.

I went back later, learned enough to pass the thirteen-word-per-minute test, upgraded to General, and promptly forgot almost all of it. Morse had felt like a licensing barrier, not something I wanted to use.

I was wrong about that. CW is awesome.

## Why CW is useful — 5:15

CW works well for weak signals, noisy conditions, and low power. A little radio, a small battery, and a simple wire antenna can reach a long way. Voice can do that too, but CW gives us another useful option when conditions are not cooperating.

It is also quiet. I can operate with headphones in places where a voice contact would be disruptive. Most importantly for me, it fits portable operating.

When I returned to amateur radio, my children found some old radio equipment and became curious. We wanted to get out and do things rather than sit in the basement. Programs such as Parks on the Air and Summits on the Air gave us a reason to take radio into interesting places.

I started learning CW in March 2026, made my first contact on April 12, and completed my first all-CW POTA activation two weeks later. I am not presenting this as someone who has done it for forty years. If a routine exchange turns into someone’s life story, I can still freeze. But the beginning of the journey is fresh in my mind, including the mistakes.

## Weak signals, narrow filters, and small stations — 7:45

CW can cut through noise well. On the receiver side, a narrow filter rejects noise surrounding the tone we want to hear. With evenly distributed background noise, a three-kilohertz filter admits about 7.8 dB more noise than a five-hundred-hertz filter. Compared with fifty hertz, the arithmetic difference is almost 18 dB.

That does not mean CW is automatically 18 dB better than SSB. Real signals, fading, tuning, and human decoding are more complicated. The useful point is that a narrow filter can remove a lot of noise surrounding a CW signal.

Small CW radios also run from small batteries. The whole station can fit in a small bag. It is quiet enough to use around other people, and the physical rhythm of a key can simply be fun.

## Four Technician HF windows — 9:58

For a Technician, CW unlocks a much larger part of HF. Ten meters is excellent, but it is not open all the time. CW also gives a Technician transmitting privileges on 80, 40, and 15 meters.

Those bands behave differently at different times. Forty meters, for example, is often regional during the day and can reach much farther at night. Eighty meters changes with the time of day too. Using CW lets a Technician begin experiencing those differences before upgrading.

The Technician CW portions are not obscure empty corners. They are places where people are already operating. A General or Extra upgrade opens much more spectrum, but you do not have to wait for an upgrade to start exploring HF.

## Practice can teach the wrong thing — 12:20

The question is: how do you avoid memorizing the cassette?

One major trap is counting dits and dahs or using a visual lookup chart. Another is making a heroic training plan—perhaps an hour at a time, several times a day—that you abandon after a few days. A third is waiting until you feel perfect before getting on the air.

We practice to use the radio, not to become perfect at drills.

At very low speeds, counting feels natural. Instead, play each character fast enough that it becomes one sound. For me that can mean a character speed around thirty words per minute, even though I cannot copy whole sentences at that speed. Extra space between the characters gives my brain time to process them without deforming each character’s rhythm.

That is Farnsworth spacing: preserve the sound of the character and slow the overall exercise with space. The exact character speed differs from person to person. If you notice yourself counting, increase it until counting is inconvenient.

## Classes add people and accountability — 15:52

You can learn a great deal with apps, but an app cannot supply camaraderie or notice everything you are doing.

The Long Island CW Club offers many small online classes at different levels and times. You can choose a class that works with your schedule and drop in. CW Academy, run by CWops, uses a different structure: the same small group meets twice each week for a term and practices between sessions.

Some people need flexibility. Others need a commitment on the calendar and the same group holding them accountable. Both approaches can work. The important thing is to choose the structure you can honestly sustain.

## Match the tool to your available attention — 18:12

Instead of asking for the single best app, ask what kind of practice fits the time you actually have.

When you can focus on a screen, Morse Mania and `learncw.vailmorse.com` make practice interactive and game-like. They play a sound, ask you to identify it, and add characters as you improve.

When you cannot look at a screen—while driving, walking the dog, or doing errands—Morse Code Ninja supplies long-form audio, podcast, and video lessons. It introduces characters gradually, plays them in changing combinations, and gives the answer immediately so you can try to recognize the sound before the voice does.

Some tools also let you connect a key and practice sending. Receiving has been the harder half for me, but listening and sending reinforce one another.

## A small practice plan — 20:58

The least impressive and most effective plan I know is simply to practice for a few minutes every day.

Do not begin with an hour-long heroic commitment. Your attention will fade, the misses will become frustrating, and you will stop absorbing the material. Fifteen minutes is a useful default because it is small enough to repeat. It can also be split into shorter sessions.

I used the Vail Morse learner daily beginning in March. The streak and game-like feedback kept bringing me back, and I made my first QSO in April. The app was useful, but the important part was returning each day.

Most trainers begin with two characters and add another as recognition improves. Do not wait for 100 percent accuracy before adding one. Missing a small amount keeps the exercise challenging and makes misses feel normal rather than catastrophic.

Practice briefly, listen on the air, and find a person willing to work with you.

## Do not wait for perfect copy — 23:33

When should you get on the air? Before you feel perfect.

For a small, planned first contact, recognize and send your own callsign, know the expected exchange, and know how to ask for help. `AGN?` asks for a repeat. `QRS PSE` asks the other operator to slow down. A missed character is not evidence that the contact failed. Repeats are part of radio.

## Why POTA is a good first classroom — 24:05

I love Parks on the Air because it gets people active and gives them a reason to get on the radio. It also creates a limited, predictable CW exchange.

The activator calls CQ. The hunter sends a callsign. The activator sends that callsign back, followed by a signal report and usually a location. The hunter replies with a report and location. Then the contact ends.

As a beginner, you can listen through repeated contacts and learn the activator’s rhythm. You do not need to decode every character immediately. You need to recognize when the activator sends your callsign and understand the shape of the exchange.

## Find a signal before transmitting — 25:12

The safest first move is listening.

Use the POTA spots page to find a nearby activation and listen through several contacts. If you do not yet have an HF radio or antenna, use a WebSDR somewhere likely to hear the activation. Recorded activation videos are another option; several operators publish minimally edited field sessions that let you hear the exchange repeat in real conditions.

As a hunter, listen for the activator, wait until they invite calls, send your callsign, listen for it to come back, exchange the expected information, and log the contact. Hunting is easier than activating because you have one main decision: when to send your call.

## The POTA exchange — 26:56

The slide shows the complete exchange. `potacw.guide` walks through it interactively and provides a reference you can keep beside your logger. I used it during my first activation, switching between the guide and my logging app whenever I wondered what to send next.

The activator sends `CQ POTA`, their callsign, and `K`, meaning they are ready to listen. The hunter sends a callsign. The activator repeats the callsign and sends a report. The hunter sends a report and location. `BK` hands the exchange back to the other station. The contact ends with thanks and a friendly dit dit.

The exact wording varies. The point is that the exchange is short enough to prepare and repeat. If you miss something, ask for that piece again and continue.

## My arranged first contact — 29:37

My first CW QSO was planned—at least partly.

I had been activating a park on voice when Brian, K1NW, heard me and realized he was nearby. He came to the park, and we spent time operating and talking. I told him I was learning CW but had not made a contact.

He was about to travel through the Southeast. He told me to listen for him and send my callsign. He would slow down, repeat anything I missed, and stay with me until we completed the contact.

I eventually heard him at a park in North Carolina. I sent my callsign incorrectly three times. He worked through repeats with me, including question marks I did not yet know how to send. The contact probably took five minutes and felt much longer, but it went in the log.

That experience took away some of the fear. Patient activators commonly slow down and repeat information for a caller who needs help. Fading can still end a contact, but a request for a repeat does not make you an inconvenience.

## Fourteen days later — 32:17

Two weeks after that first contact, I went to Scarborough North State Beach and attempted an all-CW POTA activation.

I made thirteen contacts with five watts and a vertical antenna near the salt water. It took about two hours, partly because I was nervous. I probably sent the question mark more often than any character in my callsign. It was still a successful activation.

No operator starts out already knowing how to operate CW. Everyone has had to learn it. Most people remember that and are welcoming to someone making an honest attempt.

## The complete station — 33:39

What do you actually need? Not much.

A complete portable station has a radio, a key or paddle, an antenna, power, and a log. The equipment on the table represents different tradeoffs. There are inexpensive low-power radios, compact all-mode radios, and specialized CW-only radios. A battery that fits in one hand can run a small field station.

If you already have an HF radio that supports CW and accepts a key, you may only need the key and its cable. Borrow equipment first if you can. You will learn more from a few contacts than from trying to predict every feature you might eventually want.

I prefer a paddle. The electronic keyer in the radio creates consistently timed dits and dahs while my hand chooses the pattern. The Zippy paddle is small, open-source, and inexpensive to build; I brought a batch to give to people who wanted to begin practicing.

## POTA is a gateway — 35:27

POTA is the part of amateur radio I enjoy most. I am working toward activating every POTA reference in Rhode Island during 2026, and I had activated another one on the way to HamXposition. I also mentioned an upcoming statewide activation event and wandered briefly into the geography of the remaining parks.

But POTA is not the end of the path for me. A regular QSO still has a familiar pattern, but the exchange is less constrained: perhaps a name, location, radio, antenna, or the weather. I have made a few of those contacts, and they still make me nervous. I would eventually like to have a comfortable, free-form conversation in CW.

The next step does not have to be a twenty-minute ragchew. Keep most of the structure familiar and add one human detail at a time.
