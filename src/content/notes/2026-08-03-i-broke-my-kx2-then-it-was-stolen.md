At 11:20 Monday morning, UPS marked my repaired
[Elecraft KX2](/radio/equipment/elecraft-kx2/) as delivered and photographed
the box in a clear plastic rain bag beside my roadside mailbox.

![UPS delivery email showing the Elecraft package beside my mailbox at 11:20 a.m.](/images/radio/2026-08-03-i-broke-my-kx2-then-it-was-stolen/ups-delivery-email.png)

Less than 20 minutes later, when I went outside, the radio was gone. Across the
street, I found what appeared to be the same plastic bag, empty and lying in the
rain.

![Empty clear rain bag found across the street after the KX2 was marked delivered](/images/radio/2026-08-03-i-broke-my-kx2-then-it-was-stolen/empty-rain-bag.jpg)

The radio was only in that box because I had broken it six weeks earlier.

## I forced the knob

In early June I was fitting a new main tuning knob to my KX2. The radio itself
was only a couple of weeks old. The knob did not slide onto the shaft as easily
as I expected. Instead of stopping and figuring out why, I pushed harder.

That was a mistake, and I should have known better.

The force damaged the main VFO encoder underneath the knob. It became
mechanically loose and wobbly, and tuning with it was intermittently
unreliable. The replacement knob was also stuck on the damaged shaft. I did not
see a way to pull it off without risking more damage.

There was no mystery about what had happened and no reason to blame Elecraft or
the knob maker. I had taken a fairly new radio, encountered resistance while
installing a part, and applied more force. I felt awful about it.

The KX2 was hurt, but not completely unusable. I kept operating with it during
the [Rhode Island-to-Florida POTA rove](/projects/2026-06-rove-to-fl/), including
walking and picnic-table activations at John Heinz National Wildlife Refuge,
Savannah National Wildlife Refuge, Jekyll Island, and Ross Prairie. I mostly
avoided the damaged main encoder and used the smaller **OFS/B** knob to its
right. The [KX2 manual](https://ftp.elecraft.com/KX2/Manuals%20Downloads/KX2%20owner%27s%20man%20B2.pdf)
documents that control as a way to tune VFO A in coarse steps, so I could
change frequencies within a band without touching the damaged encoder.

Changing bands was different. The KX2's band-selection process requires
tapping **BAND** and then rotating the main VFO A knob. As the trip continued
and the encoder became less reliable, changing bands got harder and harder. I
could tune around normally once I reached a band, but getting there meant
carefully working the damaged control and hoping it responded.

![KX2 and 25-foot whip on the beach at Jekyll Island while the main VFO encoder was damaged](/images/pota/2026-06-23-rhode-island-to-florida-rove-day-five/img-9406.jpg)

That experience made the damage more annoying and more instructive. The KX2
was still the radio I wanted for small field setups. Every time I worked around
the loose encoder, I was reminded that the problem existed only because I had
not stopped when the knob resisted.

The timing left me with a narrow repair window. I had wanted the KX2 for the
Florida trip, so I chose to use it damaged rather than send it away beforehand.
I also wanted it for Huntsville in August and for a work trip during the last
week of the month. Once I was home from Florida, I needed to get it repaired and
back quickly if it was going to make those trips.

## Sending it back to Elecraft

After the trip, I emailed Elecraft support and described exactly what I had
done. I told them that the encoder was badly wobbly, that the knob was stuck,
and that I was afraid to remove it myself.

Elecraft issued a Return Service Authorization, or RSA. The useful thing to
know about their process is that the authorization comes before the shipment.
Their [repair instructions](https://elecraft.com/pages/elecraft-repair-center)
say to contact support first because Elecraft has more than one repair
location. The RSA supplies the correct address, packing instructions, current
service information, and paperwork that travels with the radio.

For my KX2, the instructions said to remove the internal KXBT2 battery and the
external plastic Side KX cover. I left the stuck tuning knob alone because that
was the reason I had contacted them in the first place, and Elecraft had issued
the authorization knowing it was still attached. I included the completed RSA
paperwork, insured the shipment, and mailed the radio on July 14.

USPS delivered it to Elecraft on Friday, July 17. I did not immediately receive
the check-in email I was expecting, so I followed up on July 21. Support
confirmed that the KX2 and its paperwork were physically in the building and
would be entered into the technician queue.

Elecraft reported the repair complete three days later, on July 24. The
technician confirmed that I had damaged the main VFO encoder and replaced it
with part E640021, a 96-count contacting encoder. Elecraft also checked the
firmware, calibrated the radio, and confirmed that it met factory
specifications.

The total was $252.45: $187.50 for diagnosis and repair, $0.95 for the encoder,
and $64 for return shipping. There is something bracing about a repair where
the part costs less than a dollar and the lesson costs the other $251.50.

In my experience, Elecraft handled the repair well and moved quickly. The radio
went from confirmed receipt to repaired in three days. They accepted my account
of a self-inflicted problem without making the exchange uncomfortable, fixed
what I had broken, and checked the rest of the radio before sending it home.

There was one shipping discrepancy. I selected insured UPS 2-Day service on
the RSA, but the invoice described the $64 shipment as UPS Ground, and UPS
carried it as Ground. I noticed, but by then my main concern was getting the
repaired radio back safely.

## A delivery that disappeared

UPS picked up the KX2 on July 27. On Monday, August 3, UPS emailed me at
10:58 a.m. with an estimated delivery window of 12:15 to 3:15 p.m. The message
said a signature would be required.

The weather was genuinely bad. The National Weather Service had Warwick and
the surrounding area under a flash-flood warning. Radar estimates said one to
three inches of rain had already fallen, with another one to two inches
possible. The clear rain bag in the delivery photograph made perfect sense.
Leaving a signature-required package inside it beside the road did not.

At 11:20, well before that window, the driver recorded the package as delivered
and took the photograph beside my mailbox. The delivery email arrived a few
minutes later. I went outside about 10 minutes after receiving it and less than
20 minutes after the recorded delivery time. There was no box. The only obvious
piece of the delivery was the empty rain bag across the street.

UPS said the package had been delivered, supplied a photograph of it beside the
road, and left me staring at an empty bag. Someone took the radio during the
short time between the delivery scan and my walk to the mailbox. I was shocked,
angry, and anxious all at once. I was preparing to call the police, file a
report, and start the insurance process.

Then I looked at the live tracking page. At 11:54 it did not show a completed
delivery. It showed an emergency-situation or severe-weather delay recorded at
11:22, said the package was still on the way, and continued to say that a
signature was required. UPS also said I could not authorize release in advance
because the shipper had disabled that option.

That only made me more confused. The email and photograph said the package had
been delivered; the live tracking page said it was still on the way. I did not
know which version to trust, but the contradiction gave me some hope.

I called UPS and waited about 20 minutes for a representative. At approximately
12:30 p.m., the representative told me that UPS did not consider the package
delivered, that it remained in UPS custody, and that another delivery attempt
would be made later that day with a signature required. The representative also
told me that the driver had removed the delivery time and photograph from the
tracking record. I had already saved the delivery email, the photograph, the
later tracking state, and my photograph of the empty bag.

Only after that call did the two-minute sequence begin to make sense. One
possible explanation was that the driver had placed the box at the mailbox,
photographed and scanned it, then realized that the signature requirement
prevented completing the delivery. The driver may then have retrieved the box,
entered the weather exception, and continued the route. The loose rain bag
could have blown across the street.

UPS did not explain exactly what the driver had done, so that reconstruction
remains an inference. The important question was resolved: UPS said it still
physically possessed the package.

## Back home

The promised second attempt did not happen Monday. The KX2 finally arrived on
Tuesday, August 4, one day later.

UPS still left it without getting a signature. The tracking page had repeatedly
said the shipper required one and would not allow me to authorize release in
advance, yet the driver left the package anyway. That was not what UPS was
supposed to do, but after the previous day I was mostly relieved to have the
radio back.

![The repaired Elecraft KX2 back home after its trip to Elecraft and the confusing UPS delivery](/images/radio/2026-08-03-i-broke-my-kx2-then-it-was-stolen/repaired-kx2-home.jpg)

The KX2 had not been stolen. I still do not know exactly what the driver did on
Monday, but the most likely explanation remains that the delivery was scanned
and photographed, then reversed when the driver noticed the signature
requirement. UPS kept the package, despite briefly sending me convincing
evidence that it had been left beside the road and taken.

The first lesson is simple: if a precision control does not fit with gentle
pressure, stop. The second is less obvious. A delivery email and a
proof-of-delivery photograph can describe an event that the carrier has already
undone. When the scans disagree, their sequence matters more than the alert
that happens to reach the inbox. And apparently, **signature required** does not
guarantee that anyone will ask for a signature.

I broke the radio. Elecraft fixed it. UPS briefly convinced me that someone had
stolen it. One day later, it was finally back home---a much better ending than
the one I thought I was writing Monday morning.
