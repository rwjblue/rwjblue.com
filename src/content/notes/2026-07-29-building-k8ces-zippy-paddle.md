I am building 25 copies of the
[K8CES Zippy Paddle](https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:7255380) to give away
as part of my CW talk at the
[2026 Northeast HamXposition](https://hamxposition.org/). The event runs
August 13--16 in Marlborough, Massachusetts, and my talk is on Friday,
August 14.

K8CES published the printable model, parts links, and a compact set of assembly
instructions. This guide expands those instructions with photos, clearer part
orientation, checks at each stage, and the small details that become apparent
while assembling a batch.

The first pilot paddle is complete and working. This guide records the sequence
I will use for the rest of the batch.

## Print the parts

Before gathering the hardware, I printed the bodies and paddles in batches. I
could fit approximately six key bodies at a time: six tops and six bottoms. I
used the revised top designed for the M2 x 15 mm standoff rather than the
M2 x 16 mm standoff used by the initial version of the design.

All of these parts were printed in PETG. I used green, orange, white, and black
for the bodies. Most of the paddle pieces were black, with a smaller number
printed in white, yellow, and green. The assortment let me mix the pieces into
different color combinations while building the giveaway batch.

## Gather and organize the parts

The first step was buying and gathering all the parts. I bought enough hardware
for 25 paddles, sorted the small pieces into labeled organizer drawers, and
kept the printed tops, bases, and paddle pieces nearby.

[![Printed Zippy paddle parts arranged in front of labeled hardware organizer drawers](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/printed-parts-and-hardware-organizer.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/printed-parts-and-hardware-organizer.jpg)

I decided to build two paddles by themselves first. These are a pilot build:
working through the complete process on two units gives me a chance to learn
the sequence and find the awkward parts before setting up an assembly line for
the remaining paddles.

I counted the small hardware for those first two into a glass dish before
starting. The dish kept the bearings, inserts, screws, eyelets, standoffs,
jacks, and Allen key contained on the table and separated from the bulk
inventory.

[![Small hardware for two Zippy paddles contained in a glass dish](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/hardware-for-two-paddles.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/hardware-for-two-paddles.jpg)

Staging the two pilot paddles up front also gave me a quick inventory check: if
a count was wrong, I could correct it before the soldering iron was hot or a
half-assembled paddle was waiting for a missing part. Once I have the complete
process worked out, I can apply what I learned to the larger assembly-line
build.

## What the paddle uses

The Zippy is a small magnetic iambic paddle built from a printed top, base, and
two paddle pieces. Four bearings support the paddles. Magnets provide the
return force and hold the finished key to a steel surface. A 3.5 mm stereo jack
carries the common, dit, and dah connections to the keyer.

I purchased enough of every item on K8CES's list to build 25 complete paddles.
The counts below are the required parts, not necessarily the package quantities
sold by each supplier:

- [Bearings](https://a.co/d/5xR4NSA): 4 per paddle, 100 for the batch
- [M2 x 15 mm standoff](https://a.co/d/824YEAN): 1 per paddle, 25 for the batch
- [M2 threaded inserts for plastic](https://a.co/d/aPJKO8l): 3 per paddle, 75
  for the batch
- [M2 x 8 mm button-head screws](https://a.co/d/1pRfv7j): 3 per paddle, 75 for
  the batch
- [3.5 mm stereo jack](https://a.co/d/dvxvsuT): 1 per paddle, 25 for the batch
- [N52 neodymium magnets, 10 x 4 mm](https://a.co/d/5mujPtW): 5 per paddle,
  125 for the batch
- [M3 x 8 mm set screws](https://a.co/d/0aOZD4ey): 4 per paddle, 100 for the
  batch
- [M2 eyelet terminal](https://a.co/d/0fI9B315): 1 per paddle, 25 for the batch
- [26--28 AWG enamel-coated magnet wire](https://a.co/d/6w0NUFV): as needed
- [1.5 mm Allen key](https://a.co/d/04Ep3sdE): 1 per paddle, 25 for the batch

The five magnets have two different jobs. One goes into each paddle, with their
facing poles arranged so the magnets repel each other. That repulsion pushes
the paddles apart and provides their return force. The other three magnets go
into the bottom of the base and hold the finished key to a steel surface.

The four M3 set screws also have two jobs: two capture the wires in the
paddles, and two set the paddle travel from the sides of the top.

## Tools and supplies

After staging the parts for the two pilot paddles, I gathered the tools I would
need:

- soldering iron, solder, and flux;
- flush-cut wire cutters;
- pliers;
- a 1.5 mm tool bit;
- a hand drill fitted with a 2.1 mm bit for cleaning out the printed screw
  holes;
- a nail file for removing the enamel coating from the magnet wire; and
- the printed
  [Zippy Paddle Third Hand](https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:7347039) jig,
  designed by James Roberts.

The rest of the workbench setup includes a flat, heat-resistant surface, a
sturdy metal butter knife for pressing the front insert square, and ventilation
or fume extraction for soldering. A small helping-hands fixture would also make
the soldering steps easier, although I completed this first connection without
one. A continuity tester or multimeter, a known-good 3.5 mm stereo cable, and a
keyer are useful for testing the completed paddle.

Heat-set inserts, the soldering iron, and freshly soldered terminals remain hot
longer than they look. Neodymium magnets can also snap together hard enough to
chip, so keep the uninstalled magnets separated and under control.

## Assembly sequence

The sequence below incorporates what I learned while completing the pilot
build and finding the steps that require a little extra care.

### 1. Install the three heat-set inserts in the top

The inserts provide durable metal threads where the three M2 body screws attach
to the printed top. Each insert is a small brass sleeve with M2 threads on the
inside and knurling on the outside. Heating the brass with a soldering iron
softens the surrounding PETG as the insert is pressed into its pocket. The
plastic flows around the exterior knurling and then hardens again as it cools,
locking the insert in place. The insert is not screwed into the plastic; it is
heated and pressed straight in. Later, the body screw threads into the brass
rather than directly into the printed plastic.

Begin with the two inserts at the rear of the printed top. Use the soldering
iron to press each insert about 90 percent of the way into its hole. Turn the
top over and press it against a flat, heat-resistant surface to finish seating
both inserts. This final press makes their faces flush and keeps them aligned
with each other.

Start the single front-center insert the same way, stopping at about 90
percent. There is not enough surrounding surface to finish this one by pressing
the whole top flat. A sturdy metal butter knife worked well for me: it fit into
the opening and provided a firm, flat surface for pressing the insert the rest
of the way while keeping it square.

Before the plastic cools completely, inspect all three inserts from the side.
They should be flush, square to the part, and free of plastic inside their
threads.

[![Three M2 heat-set inserts seated in the orange printed top](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/top-with-m2-inserts.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/top-with-m2-inserts.jpg)

### 2. Install the center standoff and bearings

Thread the M2 standoff into the front-center insert in the top. Sight along it
from more than one direction and make sure it stands straight. Correcting a
slightly crooked insert or standoff now is easier than trying to pull the two
case halves together later.

Press all four bearings into their matching pockets: two in the top and two in
the base. They should sit fully in their recesses without being forced or
tilted. Installing all four at this stage keeps the small bearings together and
leaves them ready for the paddle assembly.

[![Orange printed top with the three M2 inserts, center standoff, and two top bearings installed](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/top-with-standoff-and-bearings.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/top-with-standoff-and-bearings.jpg)

### 3. Mount the jack and make the common connection

Before soldering anything, identify the three terminals on the jack. Set a
multimeter to continuity mode and plug one end of a known-good 3.5 mm TRS stereo
cable into the loose jack. At the exposed plug on the other end, the three
conductive sections are:

- **Tip:** the end of the plug;
- **Ring:** the middle section; and
- **Sleeve:** the long section nearest the cable, used as common.

Hold one meter probe against each section of the exposed plug while touching
the other probe to the jack's solder terminals. The terminal that connects to
the sleeve is common; the other two connect to tip and ring. On the jack used
for this build, the center terminal is sleeve/common and the two side terminals
are tip and ring. Look-alike jacks can arrange their terminals differently, so
confirm the mapping instead of relying only on their positions.

Feed the threaded barrel of the 3.5 mm jack through the opening from inside the
base. The jack body fits neatly into the printed recess. Thread its retaining
nut onto the barrel from the outside of the print; the jack body and nut
sandwich the printed mounting tab between them.

Before snugging the nut, rotate the jack so its center solder terminal points
toward the bottom of the base and lines up with the molded wire channel.

[![Inside of the green printed base with the jack's center solder terminal aligned with the wire channel](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/jack-inside-terminal-alignment.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/jack-inside-terminal-alignment.jpg)

[![Angled view of the 3.5 mm jack secured by its retaining nut on the outside of the printed base](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/jack-exterior-nut.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/jack-exterior-nut.jpg)

When I tried to install the M2 eyelet terminal, the button-head screw would not
pass through the printed base. A small amount of leftover print material had
narrowed the through-hole. Printing at a finer resolution might prevent this,
but I have not tested that, and the cleanup is simple enough that changing the
print settings may not be worthwhile.

I cleared the hole with a 2.1 mm bit in a hand drill. Turning it by hand made it
easy to keep the bit square and remove only the leftover material. On these
prints, 2.1 mm was the right size for the M2 button-head screws to pass through
cleanly. I checked and cleared the other M2 through-holes at the same time.

[![A 2.1 mm hand drill clearing leftover print material from an M2 screw hole in the base](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/clearing-m2-screw-hole.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/clearing-m2-screw-hole.jpg)

[![Close view of the 2.1 mm drill bit being turned by hand while clearing an M2 through-hole](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/turning-2-1mm-hand-drill.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/turning-2-1mm-hand-drill.jpg)

Put an M2 screw through the base at the eyelet-terminal position. Temporarily
thread a spare standoff onto that screw to hold the eyelet steady while
soldering. Cut a short piece of magnet wire to fit between the jack's center
terminal and the eyelet, with enough length to lie naturally in the molded
channel.

I used a nail file to scrape the enamel from the last few millimeters at both
ends of the wire. Make sure enough coating is removed for solder to wet the
exposed conductor, while leaving the rest of the wire insulated.

[![Short common wire positioned between the jack's center terminal and the eyelet held by a temporary standoff](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/common-wire-positioned.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/common-wire-positioned.jpg)

Holding the base, wire, and soldering point in position at the same time was
fiddly. A small helping-hands fixture would make this easier by holding the
pieces independently. I did not have one, so I relied on the temporary standoff
to keep the eyelet in place and worked slowly. My soldering is still a work in
progress; assembling 25 paddles should provide plenty of practice.

Solder one end of the prepared wire to the jack's center terminal and the other
to the eyelet. This wire is the common connection for both paddles.

[![Common wire soldered to the jack's center terminal and the M2 eyelet terminal](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/common-wire-soldered.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/common-wire-soldered.jpg)

After the joints cool, remove the temporary standoff. Check that the eyelet
lies flat where the standoff in the top will contact it after the case is
closed.

**Continuity check:** With the cable plugged into the jack, the sleeve should
have continuity to the M2 eyelet. The tip and ring should not have continuity
to the eyelet.

### 4. Check polarity and install the paddle magnets

Before pressing either magnet into a paddle, arrange the paddles as they will
sit in the assembled key and test the two loose magnets in that same relative
orientation. The sides that will face each other must repel. That repulsion
pushes the paddles apart and provides their return force.

Double-check this before committing either magnet to its pocket. The fit is
very tight, requires no glue, and should be treated as permanent. Once a magnet
is pressed in, there is no practical way to pull it back out without risking
damage to the paddle.

I installed the first pair backward, with the magnets attracting each other. I
had not yet thought through how the mechanism worked, so the paddles pulled
together instead of pushing apart. Verify the loose pair, keep track of their
orientation, and only then press one magnet into each paddle.

[![Two black printed paddles with their magnets pressed into the friction-fit pockets](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/paddle-magnets-installed.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/paddle-magnets-installed.jpg)

### 5. Fit a wire to each paddle

Seat both paddles in the bearings in the base. The first paddle is easy to
position, but the second becomes awkward as soon as the installed magnets begin
forcing the pair apart. James Roberts's
[Zippy Paddle Third Hand](https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:7347039) jig slides
over the front of both paddles and holds them together against that magnetic
force. It came in extremely handy here.

The same printed paddle part is used on both sides, so it has a wire hole at
both ends. In the installed orientation, feed a length of enamel-coated wire
into the hole in the top of each paddle. Continue until the wire has passed the
intersecting hole in the center of the paddle.

Insert an M3-0.5 x 8 mm grub screw into the center hole and thread it slowly
through the printed plastic. The screw captures the wire as it advances. Stop
when the flat end of the grub screw appears on the far side of the paddle; the
final contact adjustment happens after the key is assembled. Take care not to
overtighten or strip the printed threads, then give the wire a gentle tug to
confirm that it is retained. Repeat for the second paddle.

[![Orange Zippy Paddle Third Hand jig holding both paddles together while their contact wires are captured by the grub screws](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/third-hand-jig-holding-wired-paddles.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/third-hand-jig-holding-wired-paddles.jpg)

An earlier version of K8CES's instructions suggested winding two or three small
loops in each wire immediately behind the paddle. I did not add those loops.
K8CES has since told me that he no longer uses them either. Instead, I left
gentle slack in each wire so it would not pull on the paddle or interfere with
its movement.

### 6. Route and solder the paddle wires

With the jig keeping the paddles seated in the bottom bearings, route each wire
along the outside of its paddle and toward the back of the base. Leave enough
slack for the paddle to move without the wire pulling against it, but avoid
leaving a large amount of excess inside the case.

[![Both paddle contact wires routed along the outside edges toward the 3.5 mm jack while the orange jig holds the paddles together](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/paddle-wires-routed-to-jack.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/paddle-wires-routed-to-jack.jpg)

Prepare the ends with the nail file as before, then solder one paddle wire to
each of the two remaining side terminals on the 3.5 mm jack. This was another
fiddly operation where a helping-hands fixture would have made positioning the
base, paddle, wire, and jack terminal easier, but it was manageable without
one.

[![One paddle supported above the base while its contact wire is positioned near a side terminal of the 3.5 mm jack](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/paddle-contact-wire-soldering.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/paddle-contact-wire-soldering.jpg)

Trim only the confirmed excess after both solder joints are complete.

**Continuity check:** One side terminal of the jack should have continuity to
the grub screw that captures the wire in one paddle. The other side terminal
should have continuity to the grub screw in the other paddle. Neither paddle
wire should have continuity to the sleeve/common terminal while the paddles are
not touching the center standoff.

Move each paddle through its expected travel before closing the case. The
wires should move freely without pulling the paddles sideways, impeding their
return, or contacting the case seam.

### 7. Join the top and base

Fitting the top to the base is a little fiddly. Align both paddles with the
bearings in the top, then lower the top gradually while working both paddle
shafts into their bearing openings. Small adjustments to one paddle and then
the other may be necessary before both are seated. Do not force the halves
together.

Keep checking the wire routing as the gap closes. The wires should retain their
gentle slack without crossing the case seam, bunching under the standoff, or
becoming trapped beside a bearing. When both paddle shafts and all of the wires
are in the right places, the top should settle until it sits flush with the
base.

### 8. Install the three M2 body screws

Install the three M2 x 8 mm button-head screws from the bottom. The two outer
screws should line up with the threaded inserts in the top. The center screw
must line up with the M2 standoff; I had to maneuver the standoff slightly
while starting the screw to get the two threads aligned.

The center screw completes the common electrical path by pulling the top
standoff against the eyelet terminal. It needs to be snug enough for reliable
contact, but none of the three screws should be overtightened. Cranking them
down can distort the printed body or change how freely the paddles move.

[![Partially joined Zippy Paddle body with the center button-head screw aligned to the brass standoff](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/aligning-center-standoff-and-screw.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/aligning-center-standoff-and-screw.jpg)

**Continuity check:** After the center screw is snug, the sleeve of the plugged-in
cable should have continuity to the center body screw and brass standoff. The
tip and ring should remain open to the sleeve while both paddles are at rest.

### 9. Set the paddle travel

Install one M3 set screw through each side of the top: one from the left and
one from the right. These screws set how far the paddles can travel. Leaving a
side screw farther out gives that paddle more travel; threading it farther in
shortens the travel. Adjust each side in small increments until the movement
feels comfortable.

The set screws inside the paddles can also be adjusted if necessary to set the
electrical contact point. Compare both sides as you work until their spacing,
contact points, and travel feel consistent.

**Continuity check:** Sleeve-to-tip and sleeve-to-ring should both be open while
the paddles are at rest. Pressing one paddle should close only the
sleeve-to-tip circuit; pressing the other should close only sleeve-to-ring. If
either circuit is closed at rest, adjust the applicable set screw before
continuing.

[![Close side view while checking the travel and return of both black paddles](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/checking-paddle-travel.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/checking-paddle-travel.jpg)

### 10. Install the three base magnets

Check the polarity of all three remaining magnets before pressing any of them
into the base. Install them with the same pole facing outward so the finished
paddle sits evenly and grips a steel surface with all three magnets.

[![Completed orange and green Zippy Paddle with its black paddles adjusted](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/completed-zippy-paddle.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/completed-zippy-paddle.jpg)

### 11. Test the finished paddle

Repeat the complete continuity check before connecting the finished paddle:

- Sleeve to the center body screw should always have continuity.
- Sleeve to tip and sleeve to ring should both be open at rest.
- Pressing one paddle should close only sleeve to tip.
- Pressing the other paddle should close only sleeve to ring.

Connect the paddle to a keyer and verify the left/right assignment, clean
closure, comfortable travel, and reliable return. Adjust the side stop screws
in small increments if needed, then repeat both the continuity and functional
tests.

[![Completed Zippy Paddle connected to a keyer with a 3.5 mm stereo cable](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/finished-paddle-connected-to-keyer.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-07-29-building-k8ces-zippy-paddle/finished-paddle-connected-to-keyer.jpg)

That completes the paddle. The first pilot build gave me a reliable sequence
for the remaining giveaway batch and identified the places where a little
patience—or the printed third-hand jig—makes the job much easier.
