I finally built a 20-meter dipole, and I am pretty happy with how it came out.
It packs into a small bundle, deploys the way I want, and worked well on both
SSB and CW in the field.

I have wanted to build a linked dipole for a while. That is still where I want
to go, but starting with one band seemed like a good way to work out the
center, wire handling, and tuning before adding links.

The most interesting part of this build turned out to be how it packs away.

[![The complete orange dipole center and two loaded wire winders nested together in one hand](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/packed-dipole.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/packed-dipole.jpg)

## One winder for each leg

I do not enjoy deploying a dipole with both legs stored on the same winder.
What I want to do is put the center where it belongs, then walk out one leg in
each direction. Keeping the two legs on separate winders makes that much
easier.

[SOTABEAMS' Band Hopper](https://www.sotabeams.co.uk/band-hopper-ii-two-band-20m-40m-portable-dipole-antenna-system/)
uses separate element winders, which is the general
idea I wanted. I have seen one in person, though, and the packed size was
larger than I wanted for the kit I carry in on foot.

I designed a small
[BNC dipole center](https://www.printables.com/model/1838086-bnc-dipole-center),
with wire strain relief on either side and a hole for the support line. It is
essentially a little T-shaped feedpoint.

For the two winders, I started with
[K6ARK's](https://www.qrz.com/db/K6ARK)
[wire winder design](https://www.printables.com/@K6ARK/models) and added small
locating pegs. Those fit into matching holes in the center piece, so the
winders nest against it for storage. Silicone cable ties hold the packed
assembly together. Each leg still has its own winder when it is time to deploy.

[![Two orange printed winders with locating pegs beside the matching dipole center before assembly](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/printed-center-and-winders.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/printed-center-and-winders.jpg)

I tried a few versions before getting to this arrangement. The fit works
reasonably well, and the whole thing is small enough to sit in my hand. There
is room to refine it, but it already handles the packing problem I wanted to
solve.

## The wire and end insulators

I cut about 17 feet of wire for each leg and left roughly six inches folded
back at the ends so I could adjust the length in the field.

The printed
[end insulators](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1727780-wire-antenna-end-insulator#profileId-1834774)
do two jobs: small holes provide strain relief for the antenna wire, and a
larger hole gives me somewhere to clip a support line. They are simple little
parts, but they make the ends easier to manage.

[![An orange printed end insulator with small wire strain-relief holes and a larger attachment hole, held between two fingers](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/end-insulator.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/end-insulator.jpg)

At the center, I soldered the legs directly to the BNC connector. That works
for this version, but I would probably change it in a later one. If I break a
wire, replacing it means desoldering and resoldering at the connector.

For a later version, I would like each leg to terminate on a small stud,
probably M3 hardware, with a short connection from there to the BNC. That would
make the antenna wire easier to replace without reworking the feedpoint.

## Tuning it in the deployed shape

On September 12, I took the dipole to Carolina Trout Pond in
[Carolina Wildlife Management Area, US-7716](/radio/pota/US-7716/).
My brother, [Jon (KC1YED)](https://www.qrz.com/db/KC1YED), joined me with his
dog Penny. We activated together as part of the
[Activate All RI weekend](https://ripota.org/activate-ri-2026/), a statewide
effort to activate every Rhode Island POTA park over one weekend.

I hung the antenna as an inverted V from a tree. I estimated the center was
around 40 feet up, with both wire ends well off the ground.

My first analyzer check was before I had spread the legs out properly. They
were coming down steeply, with a fairly narrow angle between them, and the
antenna looked electrically too short. I was surprised by how much the
reading changed once I spread the legs farther apart and raised the ends.

[![The small orange dipole center suspended below a tree limb, with the wire legs spreading outward and the feed line hanging down](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/inverted-v-deployment.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/inverted-v-deployment.jpg)

With the legs in their operating positions, the SWR minimum was around
14.3 MHz. I let out about three inches of the folded-back wire to lengthen the
antenna slightly. That moved the minimum down to **14.135 MHz at 1.09:1**. At
**14.315 MHz**, the analyzer read **1.42:1**.

[![Antenna analyzer sweep showing a best SWR of 1.09 at 14.135 MHz and 1.42 at the 14.315 MHz sweep center](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/swr-sweep.jpg)](/images/radio/2026-09-13-compact-20-meter-dipole/swr-sweep.jpg)

I was seeing about 1.5:1 or better across the 20-meter band in this deployment.
The radio's SWR display was generally around 1.0:1 to 1.1:1 while operating.

The useful lesson for me was to finish arranging the antenna before deciding
what to do with the wire length. I expect a similar installation to behave
similarly, but I still want to check it at other heights and leg angles.

## Putting it on the air

During the activation, I got good signal reports from stations in several
directions. I worked Colorado from Rhode Island on SSB at
75 watts, then switched to CW at 50 watts and continued making good contacts.

I still want to make the wire connections easier to service and eventually
add links for more bands. For now, I have a compact 20-meter dipole that I
built myself and enjoyed using.
