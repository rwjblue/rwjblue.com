I spent too much time considering parks for an early-morning activation, then
made the simplest choice: go back to nearby
[JL Curran State Park, US-6992](/radio/pota/US-6992/). I already knew the walk,
the operating area, and the trees. That removed the stress and left more of the
morning for what I actually wanted to do: put up an antenna, operate CW, and
enjoy being outside.

This was my first full POTA activation with the tiny
[Elecraft KH1](/radio/equipment/elecraft-kh1/). I had used the radio for quick
Reverse Beacon Network checks before, but I had not yet built a complete
activation around it. The familiar site made a good place to learn the radio
while continuing to test my
[homebrew 58-foot doublet](/radio/equipment/homebrew-58-foot-doublet/).

The KH1 made the first 25 contacts on 40 and 30 meters. I then switched to the
[Elecraft KX2](/radio/equipment/elecraft-kx2/) after reaching the limit of the
KH1's tuner and made eight more on 20 meters. The final result was 33 CW
contacts across the three bands.

## At a glance

- **Where:** [JL Curran State Park, US-6992](/radio/pota/US-6992/), Rhode Island; Leyton Knight Road parking area before the bend
- **When:** August 12, 2026; contacts from 7:46 to 10:11 a.m. EDT
- **Activation:** 33 CW QSOs on 40, 30, and 20 meters; 25 of them with the KH1
- **Radios:** [Elecraft KH1](/radio/equipment/elecraft-kh1/) on 40 and 30 meters; [Elecraft KX2](/radio/equipment/elecraft-kx2/) on 20 meters
- **Paddle:** KH1's built-in paddle at first, then a [VK3IL pressure paddle](/radio/equipment/vk3il-pressure-paddle/) from VE6LK
- **Antenna:** [Homebrew 58-foot portable doublet](/radio/equipment/homebrew-58-foot-doublet/) with two 29-foot legs and 28 feet of balanced feed line
- **Geometry:** Center about 26 or 27 feet high; both ends at least 5 feet high; radiator axis roughly 275/100 degrees

## A familiar walk and a higher center

As far as I can tell, JL Curran has four separate parking areas and many
possible routes through the woods. This time I used the parking area on Leyton
Knight Road before the bend, then followed a roughly half-mile route along a
trail I had not taken before. I ended up among old stone walls and boulders,
with plenty of tall trees for the antenna. There was no picnic table or water
view, but it was quiet, which was exactly what I needed after debating longer
drives and unfamiliar setups.

I got the doublet's center almost to the end of the throw line, approximately
26 or 27 feet above the ground. Both radiator ends remained at least five feet
high, perhaps a little more. One leg ran roughly west at 270 to 280 degrees and
the other roughly east at 100 degrees. The alignment and heights are estimates,
but this was a cleaner and higher installation than some of my earlier tests.

## Learning the KH1 in the field

The first full activation also meant learning several parts of the KH1's small
interface while I was using it. Receive volume and CW monitor volume are
separate: tapping the AF/MON knob switches it to monitor level, while holding
the same knob enters SPOT. That made it possible to set a comfortable sidetone
without changing the received-signal level.

The VFO knob was easy to bump while keying. Holding BAND/MODE and then holding
button 4 locks the VFO and XIT, while leaving RIT adjustable. I also learned to
read the normal `C +.00` display correctly: `C` is CW mode, `+.00` is the shared
RIT/XIT offset, and the absence of a trailing `R` or `X` means neither function
is active.

I settled on three programmable functions for portable operating:

- **PF1 / `VBT` - VBAT:** Show internal-battery voltage and amp-hours
  consumed.
- **PF2 / `CLR` - RIT CLEAR:** Immediately zero the shared RIT/XIT offset.
- **PF3 / `PAN` - PAN MODE:** Start the KH1's scan and mini-pan display.

The amp-hour counter had not been reset after the last full charge, so its
value was not useful during this outing. The repeatable habit is to reset it to
zero only after a full charge; it then remains available across operating
sessions as an approximate measure of energy used.

## The KH1 on 40 and 30 meters

I began on 40 meters with the KH1. Aside from one or two contacts during
earlier tuning checks, this was the first real 40-meter test of the doublet.
The KH1's tuner found a 1.2 to 1.3:1 match without trouble and the radio
indicated the expected five watts. After trying a couple of frequencies, I
settled at 7.068 MHz for the main run. I made 21 contacts before moving to 30
meters shortly before 9 a.m.

The Reverse Beacon Network showed a solid regional footprint on 40 meters:
reports from ten skimmers extended from roughly 80 to 570 miles, generally
between 7 and 16 dB above the decoding threshold. It was reassuring evidence
that five watts and the doublet were getting out normally.

I started with the paddle built into the KH1 and used it long enough to learn
that it was not comfortable for me over an extended run. I am not sure whether
the problem was the way I held the radio or the extra finger pressure needed to
trigger its switches, but the old repetitive-strain symptoms in my right hand
began to flare up. That is about my hand and years of poor typing posture, not
an apparent fault in the KH1 paddle. I switched to my
[VK3IL pressure paddle](/radio/equipment/vk3il-pressure-paddle/), built and
sold by VE6LK, which was much more comfortable for sustained operating.

![A closer view of the KH1, external paddle, and orange feed line at the wooded operating position](/images/pota/2026-08-12-jl-curran-kh1-doublet/2-photo-2.jpg)

Thirty meters was more curious. The KH1 again tuned the doublet to about 1.2:1,
but its display showed only about three power bars during ordinary CQs. This
was not the intentionally lower-power ATU carrier, and the transmitter was set
to HI. The VBT display read about 11.4 to 11.5 volts at rest. The separate
V/I/temperature display showed 11.0 to 11.2 volts while sending, current around
0.8 amp, and an internal temperature of 42 C. That second voltage is measured
after an internal isolation diode and normally reads about 0.2 volt lower than
VBT, in addition to any sag under load.

Those measurements did not point to a weak battery, high SWR, or thermal
limiting. The KH1 stores its power alignment by band, actual output varies with
supply voltage and load impedance, and Elecraft notes that component variation
can keep some bands from quite reaching five watts. Even if the three bars
accurately represented three watts, the difference from five watts is only
about 2.2 dB. It was worth noting, but not worth interrupting the activation or
changing calibration in the field.

I made four contacts on 30 meters. RBN reports there were weaker, mostly 4 to 9
dB, although they reached from the Northeast into the Midwest and as far as
Oklahoma during an earlier call. Increasing activity around the Wednesday CWT
start also made finding a comfortable frequency more difficult.

## Where the KH1 tuner stopped

An interesting result came when I tried the higher bands. The KH1 could not
find a usable match on the unchanged doublet: its best result was approximately
8.3:1 on 20 meters and 6.7:1 on 17 meters. I did not transmit at those SWR
levels.

Instead, I swapped in the KX2. Its wider-range tuner matched the same antenna on
20 meters, and I made eight more five-watt CW contacts at 14.063 MHz. That
strongly suggests the antenna and feed line were not failing; the impedance at
the radio was simply outside the KH1 tuner's matching range on those bands.

| Band | Radio | CW QSOs |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| 40 meters | KH1 | 21 |
| 30 meters | KH1 | 4 |
| 20 meters | KX2 | 8 |
| **Total** |  | **33** |

The doublet itself continues to be a useful portable antenna. It fit among the
trees, tuned easily with the KH1 on 40 and 30 meters, and worked on 20 once the
KX2 took over. Before touching the KH1's per-band power alignment, I want to
compare 40 and 30 meters into a 50-ohm dummy load with the ATU bypassed and an
external wattmeter. That should distinguish a real output difference from a
display-calibration difference. I also want to try a different feed-line
length to see whether it can place 20 or 17 meters inside the KH1 tuner's
matching range.

For a first real POTA outing with the KH1, this was a useful success. The simple
local choice delivered exactly what I wanted: a quiet walk, a low-stress setup,
25 contacts with the new radio, and eight more after a quick KX2 swap.
