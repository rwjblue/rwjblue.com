My original
[Flight of the Bumblebees plan](/notes/2026-07-26-flight-of-the-bumblebees/)
was deliberately simple: choose a nearby Rhode Island park, put a resonant
dipole in a tree, and spend Sunday afternoon operating five-watt CW.

Then my wife suggested that we make a day trip to Block Island.

That is a much better outing, but it changes the radio problem. I now have an
opportunity to activate
[Block Island National Wildlife Refuge, US-0513](/radio/pota/US-0513/), one of
the Rhode Island POTA references I still need. Before I carry a mast and sixty
feet of wire onto a ferry, however, I need to identify a location that is both
inside the reference and practical for a portable antenna.

## The whole island is not the park

My first thought was to operate somewhere near North Light or Sandy Point. The
[USFWS visitor information](https://www.fws.gov/refuge/block-island/visit-us)
says the refuge is about four miles from the ferry landing and describes the
walk to North Light as being bordered by refuge lands. That does not mean the
entire beach, lighthouse area, or northern end of the island is automatically
inside the POTA reference.

The official USFWS boundary data shows a collection of disconnected parcels.
Some are near the northern tip, while others are inland along Corn Neck Road.
The map below renders the current USFWS National Realty Boundaries GeoJSON for
the refuge.

This is useful evidence for deciding whether a coordinate is inside US-0513,
but it is not an access map. USFWS describes the data as resource-grade and
warns that not every mapped area is open to visitors. A valid POTA position
still needs lawful public access, a safe place to stop, and compliance with any
on-site closures.

## A promising precedent on Skipper's Island Road

[WB4SON's 2023 Block Island report](https://wb4son.com/wpblog/?p=4890) provides
a much more promising lead than the exposed beach. Previous activators had
used the transfer-station parking lot without being certain that it was inside
the refuge. WB4SON and W1LY instead found that
[Skipper's Island Road](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=41.2031732%2C-71.5717162)
passes directly through one of the disconnected refuge parcels.

They operated there successfully. W1LY supported a 40-meter end-fed wire as an
inverted V from a jack-kite pole, while WB4SON used a 17-foot whip for 20-meter
CW. That establishes two important things: there is at least one inland
location that has been used for a defensible US-0513 activation, and a
center-supported wire antenna is practical there.

The current USFWS GeoJSON also places the mapped Skipper's Island Road location
inside the refuge's fee-owned parcel. What I do not yet know is where they
stopped, whether there is a public shoulder or clearing suitable for someone
arriving without a vehicle, and whether a convenient tree is available without
obstructing the road or approaching the neighboring homes. I plan to ask the
RI-POTA group for first-hand advice rather than treating a polygon intersection
as permission to set up anywhere along the road.

## Dipole first, mast second

I still prefer the linked 40/20-meter dipole. It gives me 20 meters for the
main daytime FOBB activity and 40 meters for closer stations later in the
afternoon. It is also an antenna I can understand completely and repair in the
field.

The preferred deployment is a tree-supported inverted V:

- hoist the feedpoint approximately 20 to 30 feet;
- run the coax vertically down from the center;
- slope both legs to end cords kept safely above head height; and
- use the banana-plug links to change between 20 and 40 meters.

If the parcel has no suitable branch, one of my fiberglass masts can support
the center in the same basic arrangement WB4SON described. The dipole legs can
provide two directions of support, with a third nonconductive guy completing
the mast support. That needs more footprint than a tree-supported antenna, but
it remains more attractive than an exposed vertical if I can find a safe
clearing.

The KJ6ER Challenger is now the backup rather than the primary antenna. It can
put me on 20 meters quickly, but its long whip needs solid ground staking in
real wind. I do not want to discover at the refuge that stakes are inappropriate
or that the available ground cannot hold them.

## Ferry and weather constraints

The published
[2026 Block Island Ferry schedule](https://www.blockislandferry.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/2026schedule-FINAL.pdf)
has the Newport high-speed ferry leaving at 9:10 a.m. and the return leaving
Block Island at 5:00 p.m. The crossing is approximately one hour, and passengers
must be aboard at least fifteen minutes before departure.

That gives me plenty of time to reach the northern half of the island and
deploy before FOBB starts at 1:00 p.m. It does not allow me to operate through
the official 5:00 p.m. finish. Unless the transportation plan changes, I should
expect to go QRT around 3:45 or 4:00, tear down quickly, and return to Old
Harbor.

The current
[National Weather Service forecast](https://forecast.weather.gov/MapClick.php?FcstType=text&lat=41.161&lg=en&lon=-71.584&unit=1)
is sunny with a high near 75 degrees and an east wind around 11 to 13 mph.
That should be a pleasant day for a trip, but it reinforces the value of a
tree-supported dipole or a carefully guyed center mast.

## The remaining decision

The antenna plan is now fairly clear. I will take the linked 40/20-meter dipole,
one mast, a throw line, three guy lines, and the Challenger only if the ferry
luggage remains manageable.

The location still needs one piece of local knowledge: the precise place along
Skipper's Island Road, or another publicly accessible refuge parcel, where I
can operate without blocking access or intruding on private property. Once I
have that answer, this changes from a speculative island activation into a
straightforward portable station with an unusually good view.
