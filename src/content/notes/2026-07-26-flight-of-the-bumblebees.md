On Sunday, July 26, I plan to operate in the
[Adventure Radio Society Flight of the Bumblebees](https://ars-qrp.com/FOBB/FOBB.html).
FOBB is a four-hour QRP CW event built around portable field stations. The
portable operators are the Bumblebees, and I have been issued Bumblebee number
121.

The event runs from 1700 to 2100 UTC, or 1:00 to 5:00 p.m. here in Rhode
Island. Bumblebees send an RST, their state, and their Bumblebee number. I will
therefore send something like:

```text
559 RI 121
```

My main objective is to put together a station that is simple enough to build,
deploy, and understand. I have several convenient portable antennas, but this
event feels like a good reason to make a basic resonant dipole and see what I
can do with five watts and wire.

## Choosing a location

I have not settled on the operating location yet. Beach Pond is one
possibility, particularly if I can find a comfortable spot with enough room
and suitable trees. Another option is to activate a Rhode Island POTA
reference I have not yet completed. The dependable fallback is one of my local
parks, where I already understand the access and operating possibilities.

For a four-hour event, the operating position matters almost as much as the
radio site. I am looking for shade, a reasonable place to sit, enough separation
from other visitors, and either a useful overhead branch or room for a
telescoping mast. Cellular coverage is helpful for POTA spotting but not
essential for FOBB.

## The bands that appear to matter

FOBB permits contacts on 80, 40, 20, 15, and 10 meters, with suggested
gathering frequencies around 3.566, 7.036, 14.036, 21.036, and 28.036 MHz.
The same station can be worked again on a different band.

The official score reporting does not collect a band-by-band breakdown, so
there are no authoritative percentages for band popularity. Operator reports
nevertheless point in a consistent direction:

- 20 meters is generally the primary daytime FOBB band.
- 40 meters provides regional contacts and may become more active later.
- 15 and 10 meters are worth checking when propagation supports them, but they
  appear to carry much less FOBB activity.
- 80 meters is unlikely to be important during a midsummer afternoon.

In his
[2019 FOBB report](https://www.n4kgl.info/2019/07/), Greg N4KGL wrote that
20 meters dominated his operation. Craig WB3GCK
[reported from Pennsylvania](https://wb3gck.com/page/48/) that most early
activity was on 20 meters before 40 meters began to come alive later.

That makes a 20-meter dipole the simplest credible antenna for the event. A
linked 40/20-meter version would add useful regional coverage without changing
the feedpoint or basic deployment.

## The simplest antenna

The starting point is a center-fed 20-meter dipole cut for the FOBB activity
around 14.036 MHz. The calculated total length is about 33 feet 4 inches, or
16 feet 8 inches on each side. I will cut both legs slightly long and trim them
after measuring the installed antenna.

A small BNC-to-dual-binding-post adapter can provide the electrical
connections, but it should not carry the antenna tension. I plan to mount or
secure the adapter to a small plastic center plate with:

- a top hole for the hoisting cord;
- a strain-relief hole for each antenna leg; and
- the BNC connector facing down so the coax can descend vertically.

Each radiator will pass through its strain-relief hole before connecting to a
binding post. The hoisting cord and plastic plate will carry the load rather
than the binding posts or BNC connector.

The easiest installation is a center-supported inverted V. I can throw a line
over a branch, pull the center to approximately 20 to 30 feet, and tie the
wire ends outward with nonconductive cord. If no suitable tree is available,
the same center plate can hang from a telescoping mast, with the dipole legs
and an additional guy line supporting the mast.

## The optional 40-meter links

If time permits, I can place an insulator at the end of each 20-meter radiator
and add approximately 16 feet 7 inches of wire beyond it. Closing both links
would produce a roughly 66-foot 6-inch 40-meter dipole; opening them would
leave the original 20-meter dipole.

I will use small banana plugs for the links. Each plug and socket will provide
the electrical connection while the adjacent insulator carries the wire
tension. This adds only two extension wires and two connections, but it also
means lowering the antenna whenever I change bands. I would rather arrive with
a tested single-band dipole than rush an untested linked version.

## Operating plan

I expect to begin near 14.036 MHz on 20 meters, where the largest group of
FOBB stations is likely to gather. If I add the 40-meter extensions, I will
move near 7.036 MHz after the initial 20-meter activity slows and return to
each band at least once.

Because the operation may also be a POTA activation, I can answer ordinary
POTA hunters while sending my Bumblebee exchange. The FOBB rules explicitly
allow those contacts when the Bumblebee number is included.

The practical checklist is short:

- choose the park and confirm that the antenna layout is permitted;
- finish and test the dipole in its intended inverted-V geometry;
- pack a [throw line](/radio/equipment/throw-line-kit/) or mast, end ropes, and
  a backup way to support the center;
- bring the radio, key, battery, headphones, coax, logging gear, water, and
  insect protection;
- arrive early enough to solve antenna problems before 1700 UTC.

This is the first planning part of the experiment. The next entry follows a
last-minute change in location that replaces the dipole with a beach vertical.
After FOBB, a final field report can concentrate on the chosen location,
station configuration, band conditions, contacts, and what the revised antenna
plan teaches me.
