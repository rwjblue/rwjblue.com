Rhode Island had a fairly small group of operators for its first week in the
[ARRL America250 Worked All States event](https://www.arrl.org/america250-was).

Earlier in the summer, I had a lot of fun hunting the 13 Colonies special
event. It was my first time seriously chasing something like that, and I knew
how disappointed I would have been if one of the stations had simply never
appeared. When I saw the small Rhode Island roster, I felt bad thinking about
hunters who might spend the week looking for the state and never hear it. I did
not want to leave someone in that position when offering some of my time was
within my control.

I could not cover the whole week, and I was not trying to. I just wanted to
contribute enough time to help make the state easier to find.

Over three days, I put **W1AW/1** on the air for seven operating blocks and
logged **253 contacts**: 132 on SSB, 79 on CW, and 42 on FT8. I worked 211
different callsigns in 38 states, plus a little DX along the way.

## Planning enough coverage

I was not chasing a personal score or trying to carry Rhode Island by myself. I
signed up for blocks I thought I could sustain, with the hope that the whole
group could give hunters several chances to find the state.

The public [ARRL America250 event page](https://www.arrl.org/america250-was)
explains the bands, modes, and awards. The operator guidelines add a
coordination rule: each state may have only one CW operator, one Phone operator,
and one Digital operator active at a time, regardless of band. Two CW operators
cannot split between 40 and 20 meters, for example, but one CW, one Phone, and
one Digital station can all be on the air together.

The first schedule was coming together over email, so I put together a
[shared operating schedule](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1e_cfDKP1vhLrrNxboxHprneeaqwQATWI_i0MDcoisp0/edit?gid=2026072200#gid=2026072200).
It gave everyone one place to see the gaps and claim time without another round
of email. A dozen operators and club stations ultimately got **W1AW/1** on the
air, and between us we covered CW, Phone, and Digital. I was one person in that
group, filling some of the open blocks.

My biggest coordination takeaway is that we should start sooner next time.
[ARRL's current schedule](https://www.arrl.org/files/file/Year%20Of%20The%20Club%20-%20America250-WAS/America250-WAS-State-Schedule.pdf)
has Rhode Island returning at 0000 UTC on December 2. That gives us time to
recruit more operators, settle the schedule, and test our stations before the
second week begins. Here in Rhode Island, that start time is 7:00 PM on Tuesday,
December 1.

- **7:53–10:29 AM:** 38 contacts on 40-meter CW and SSB, then 20-meter CW and

Twenty meters was the workhorse band, accounting for 213 of my 253 contacts.
Forty meters was most useful early in the morning, while 17 meters gave me a
short FT8 opening around midday Thursday.

## Letting conditions change the plan

Wednesday morning was a good reminder that a schedule is only a starting point.
My first 40-meter CW run produced nine contacts. When I moved to 20 meters, the
CW portion of the band was full of stations in a CWops CWT. I switched to SSB
for a short run, then went back to CW after the CWT ended. I made another 31
contacts between those two runs.

Wednesday evening was my busiest block. I started with 26 FT8 contacts, made
three on CW, and then settled into 20-meter SSB. That SSB run added 74 contacts
between roughly 7:06 and 8:30 PM.

The bands often felt strange during the week, especially the fading. NOAA's
after-the-fact numbers give me at least one reason not to dismiss that feeling.
[Solar flux stayed around 148–150](https://services.swpc.noaa.gov/text/daily-solar-indices.txt),
but on Thursday the
[estimated planetary A index reached 22 and Kp briefly climbed above 5](https://services.swpc.noaa.gov/text/daily-geomagnetic-indices.txt).
That crosses NOAA's threshold for a
[G1 minor geomagnetic storm](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/node/1085), and it
happened during my 17-meter FT8 block and the later CW session.

That does not explain every rough morning. The other days were calmer, and I
was often asking 20 meters to work pretty early in the day. Still, Thursday
really was unsettled.

By the time I stopped, I had worked 38 states as well as stations in Canada,
Mexico, the Cayman Islands, and several European countries.

## Two very different receive antennas

I used the DX Commander Expedition for nearly all of my transmitting and kept a
loop on the ground available for receive. Sometimes the loop was quieter;
sometimes the vertical heard a caller better.

This was also one of the first times I had used the K4D for an extended
operation instead of a short session or test. Spending several days with it
gave me a much better feel for its receive tools.

The difference became especially useful when signals were fading. A caller
could nearly disappear on the loop while remaining readable on the vertical,
or the other way around. With the K4D's diversity receive mode, I could listen
to both paths together instead of betting the contact on whichever antenna
happened to be stronger at that moment.

## Getting CW, Phone, and FT8 ready

CW benefited the most from advance setup. After a little experimentation, I
settled on four memories:

- **M1, long CQ:** CQ WAS250 RI CQ WAS250 RI DE **W1AW/1** **W1AW/1** K
- **M2, short CQ:** CQ WAS250 RI DE **W1AW/1** K
- **M3, sign-off:** TU 73 DE **W1AW/1** E E
- **M4, event ID:** **W1AW/1** RI ARRL AMERICA250 WAS EVENT

Including the state in the CQ helped callers understand which W1AW portable
operation they had found. The shorter CQ was useful once I had established a
frequency, and M4 gave me a quick explanation when someone asked what the
operation was.

For SSB, I recorded one longer CQ memory but otherwise found it easier to talk.
Phone contacts moved quickly enough that more voice memories would have added
friction instead of removing it.

I had not planned to spend much time on digital, but
[N6PE](https://www.qrz.com/db/N6PE) reached out while trying to find Rhode
Island for Worked All States. Suddenly the gap in our digital coverage was not
just an empty cell in a schedule; there was an actual hunter hoping someone
would get on the air. That was all the nudge I needed to work through the FT8
setup.

I used WSJT-X with QK4 controlling the K4D because that combination handled
**W1AW/1** correctly; I wrote up the details separately in
[Running WSJT-X through QK4 with an Elecraft K4D](/notes/2026-07-16-wsjt-x-qk4-k4d-network-setup/).

I made 26 FT8 contacts on 20 meters Wednesday evening and another 16 on 17
meters Thursday afternoon. [N6PE](https://www.qrz.com/db/N6PE) made it into
both FT8 runs, and we also worked on SSB later Wednesday. It was a small,
concrete reminder that every open slot represented hunters who might be looking
for Rhode Island.

## One log, with tradeoffs

I kept all seven sessions in one continuing America250 activation in Ham2K
Portable Logger. It worked, and having one unified ADIF at the end was
convenient, but I am not sure it was the best choice. It made the stats for each
individual operating session harder to see. I could recover them by analyzing
the ADIF, but separate activations might have made the progression through the
week easier to follow.

Running PoLo on my Apple silicon Mac was a particularly nice surprise. The Mac
can run the iPad version directly, so I could log at the radio and have the same
activation sync with PoLo on my phone. FT8 was the rough edge: those contacts
started in WSJT-X and had to be imported into PoLo after each digital session.
I would love a more direct path between FT8 and PoLo.

The operating instructions make electronic ADIF logging and backups a critical
part of the job. My combined export has all 253 contacts with **W1AW/1** as the
station callsign, N1RWJ as the operator, and the Rhode Island location fields.
I still need to run the event's operator-side validation step before sending
the resulting file to the state coordinator, who combines the operators' logs
for ARRL and Logbook of The World.

## What worked

Short, repeatable blocks fit this event well. I could cover mornings, evenings,
and three modes without making the week feel like one continuous contest.
Changing modes when the band was crowded worked better than forcing the
original schedule.

The schedule and the other operators mattered at least as much as any one
session. This was a group effort, and several of the other operators brought
far more experience than I did. My contribution was simply to take some open
blocks and be willing to change modes when another gap appeared.

Taken together, the group gave hunters chances to find Rhode Island on CW,
Phone, and Digital. That was what I hoped we could accomplish.

## What I want to improve in December

I hope we can bring in more operators and get the schedule in front of the
group earlier. More people would give hunters more chances to find Rhode Island
while spreading the work across a wider group.

I also want every part of my station tested before the first scheduled minute:
CW memories, voice memory, logging profile, and the digital interface. My first
session started a little late while I worked out the best CW memory arrangement,
and FT8 took real-time experimentation that would be better done ahead of the
activation.

Finally, I want to decide whether to keep one week-long PoLo activation or split
the week into separate operating blocks. One activation worked, but I want the
next log to be just as easy to submit and easier to understand while the week
is still in progress.

I am glad I could contribute a few blocks. I was one operator in a broader
group, not the person carrying the activation. My part was modest: get my
station ready, cover the times I had claimed, and give some hunters another
chance to find Rhode Island.
